1. Why Faults Host Geothermal Systems

In the northwestern Great Basin and Walker Lane (the geographic extent of the GeoDAWN survey), geothermal systems are non-magmatic. Meteoric water infiltrates mountain ranges, sinks several kilometers into the upper crust, is heated by elevated regional background heat flow (~80–100 mW/m²), and ascends rapidly along permeable fault zones.

Continuous Recurrent Slip Prevents Self-Sealing

Hot thermal water carries high concentrations of dissolved minerals (silica, calcite). As fluids ascend and cool, mineral precipitation seals fracture permeability in a few thousand years. Only faults with active Quaternary deformation undergo recurrent seismic slip that breaks mineral seals and keeps fluid conduits open.

Geometric Irregularities Control 85%+ of Upflow

Comprehensive surveys by the Great Basin Center for Geothermal Energy (Faulds & Hinz, 2011, 2012) prove that geothermal upflow does not occur randomly along planar fault scarps. Over 85% of commercial systems cluster at specific geometric complexities: step-overs, terminations, intersections, and accommodation zones.

2. The Five Primary Geothermal Structural Settings

A. Step-Overs & Relay Ramps (En-Echelon Normal Faults)

When two sub-parallel normal fault strands overlap, the crust between them experiences intense dilatational strain. High-density, high-angle connecting fractures form, creating ideal vertical permeability. Examples: Steamboat Springs, Desert Peak, Brady, Tungsten Mountain.

B. Fault Terminations & Tip Damage Zones

As displacement on a normal fault drops toward its endpoint, stress concentrations generate horse-tail splay fractures that extend 500m to 1500m into adjacent valley fill. This is where hidden blind faults reside. Examples: Gerlach, San Emidio.

C. Fault Intersections

Intersections between NW-striking dextral strike-slip faults (Walker Lane) and NNE-striking normal faults (Basin and Range). Intersecting fault surfaces form vertical fracture pipes that facilitate deep convection. Example: Soda Lake, Stillwater.

D. Accommodation Zones / Polarity Reversal Belts

Belts where the regional dip direction of normal faults reverses (e.g. from west-dipping to east-dipping). Complex interlocking fracture networks form across the transfer zone. Example: Dixie Valley, McGinness Hills.

E. Pull-Apart Basins & Extensional Horsetails

Localized rifting at releasing bends along major strike-slip faults, creating deep depocenters and high heat-flow conduits.
